01

HAI-DEF: why Google gives these away

Google Research’s health AI programme has long worked on catalyzing the adoption of human-centered AI in healthcare — historically through demonstrations and internal studies with partners. Health AI Developer Foundations is the shift from demonstrating to supplying: releasing pre-trained models and supporting tools so third parties can build the applications themselves.

The licensing is the part worth underlining. Everything released under HAI-DEF carries an enabling license: free, no obligation to Google, no per-inference payment, usable offline, embeddable in commercial products. In the session’s framing, these tools are meant to be adopted and owned by the developers who use them.

The reasoning

No single organisation can do all of this. AI research institutes, cloud providers, healthcare providers and health tech companies each hold a different piece. Google supplies the layer where it has the most leverage — the foundation models — so the others can assemble the rest.

02

Three models, three jobs

MedGemma is built on Gemma, Google’s open general-purpose model, and is described as Google’s most capable open model for medical text and image comprehension. The collection splits three ways, and choosing between them is mostly a question of what you are reading and what you can afford to run.

What it gets right, and what it misses

Two worked examples were shown, both reviewed afterwards by a board-certified thoracic radiologist. The pattern in both is the same, and it is the reason for the whole “adapt, don’t deploy” message.

Longitudinal chest X-rays

Two X-rays of the same patient at different times. The prompt: is the pneumonia unchanged, worsened, or improved in the second image?

Model · correctSaid the pneumonia had worsened.

Radiologist · agreedConfirmed the pneumonia was worsened.

ButThe radiologist also noted a region of relative lucency in the mid-right lung that could represent cavitation, or merely lung spared by pneumonia — a finding the model did not surface.

Volumetric CT

An entire CT volume supplied, with the patient being evaluated for liver tumours.

Model · correctReported a large irregular mass in the right lobe of the liver.

Radiologist · agreedConfirmed the primary assessment.

ButOne of the model’s findings appeared spurious — a secondary finding that was not actually there.

Broad strokes correct in both cases; details unreliable in both. A missed finding in one, a fabricated one in the other. That is a useful, honest baseline — and precisely why the model cards carry an intended-use statement saying this is not for clinical diagnosis or management as shipped.

We consider MedGemma to be a good starting point for medical modeling, but it’s not intended to be used for clinical diagnosis or management out of the box. It’s meant to be fine-tuned and adapted by third-party users when building their own medical AI applications, and then evaluated in their particular use cases. Dan Golden, Google Research

The workflow Google expects you to follow

Six steps, but they are not equally weighted. Step three is called out as critical, and steps five and six are where the session says developers spend most of their time.

What Google supplies for each step

  • Running and evaluating on your data — example notebooks on Hugging Face and Model Garden.
  • Fine-tuning — example notebooks using Google Cloud.
  • Deploying — examples for turning a tuned model into an API via Hugging Face and Vertex AI.
  • Inspiration — demo applications with source, so you can see how MedGemma is orchestrated into something larger.
05

Gemini or MedGemma?

The honest default

If Gemini works for your use case, Gemini is probably the better choice — more convenient, no hosting, tuning APIs, and in many (not all) cases better performance. MedGemma exists for the cases where Gemini cannot work, and for medical imaging, where it has been specifically tuned.

One more difference worth planning for: a hosted model changes over time, and old versions get deprecated. That is usually an advantage. Occasionally it is disqualifying — and open weights are the only way to freeze behaviour.

06

Running both together

A question from the audience: a team already running Gemini in production wants to add MedGemma without replacing it. There are two distinct reasons to do that, and they lead to different designs.

Reason one — MedGemma as a specialist tool

Gemini fronts the application and handles general work and patient-facing flows; when a medical image appears, it farms that out to MedGemma as a tool. Clinical imaging, radiology especially, is where MedGemma has the edge. For medical text, the session’s own view is that Gemini will probably do better.

Reason two — MedGemma as a PHI boundary

The more architecturally interesting pattern. MedGemma runs inside infrastructure you control, so PHI is only ever seen there; whatever it produces is de-identified before anything leaves for Gemini.

Why open weights simplify HIPAA

The session’s clearest formulation: systems are HIPAA-compliant, models are not. MedGemma is a binary you download and deploy, not an API you send data to — so you can run it on-premise where no data leaves the hospital at all, or on Google Cloud’s HIPAA-compliant services and inherit those guarantees. Either way the question “where did my data go?” has a definite answer.

07

What it was trained on — and what it wasn’t

This is the single most practical thing to check before starting. The rule given: anything used in training is closer to in-distribution, so it performs better out of the gate and needs less adaptation data. Anything not in training, and MedGemma may give you no advantage over base Gemma.

Medical video is a partial exception

Medical and surgical video is RGB visible-light imagery, which sits closer to what Gemma and Gemini were originally trained on than X-rays do — X-rays are high bit-depth and dimensionally different. So even without specific tuning, light fine-tuning should get decent results, passing multiple frames into the prompt together. Surgical video support is being worked on for future versions.

Non-English use

An acknowledged limitation: most available training data is English. The answer given is not a prompting trick — it is fine-tuning on medical data in your own language, aligned with your intended use. The medical concepts are already in the model; it is the language layer (conceptually, not a literal layer) that needs adapting. LoRA or QLoRA is often enough, with full fine-tuning available when the use case warrants it. Tooling to make this easier is being actively developed.

The related point is worth repeating: anyone building an application needs evaluation data anyway. Take a subset of it and tune on that.

Quantization and edge deployment

  • Today: no quantization-aware-training checkpoints are published. Expect roughly a 5% performance drop after quantization depending on the benchmark — often recoverable with fine-tuning.
  • Off the shelf: third parties, Unsloth among them, publish quantized versions.
  • Coming: the next release is hoped to include QAT checkpoints, first-party quantized models, and instructions for quantizing your own fine-tuned weights. A smaller model based on Gemma 4 2B is also hoped for, aimed at mobile.

The regulatory picture

Building a medical device on MedGemma is explicitly permitted by the license. What does not yet exist is precedent.

In AI time scales, we’re pretty late in this process — we launched more than a year ago, and that’s like ten generations of AI. In FDA time scales, we’re quite early. On clearances for generative models

No FDA or equivalent clearance for a generative MedGemma application is known to the team. The one cited clearance — Nexus AI in South Africa, for chest X-ray classification — is on an earlier, non-generative model generation.

The team offers no official regulatory advice, on the reasonable grounds that the FDA has the first and last word and any specifics would date quickly. The personal view given, and it is a good one: beyond tracking your development and documentation, evaluation is what matters. Regulators want to see that the application is safe and effective for its intended use, and not likely to be used outside that intended use. Design backwards from those two claims.

Worth hearing

“It’s tough to be a pioneer — but there is a huge opportunity in using these tools for regulated medical devices. Please don’t let the lack of precedent be a deterrent.”

The shape of the advice

Start with Gemini. Evaluate it honestly against your own data. Reach for MedGemma when Gemini cannot meet a hard constraint — offline, on-premise, IP ownership, a medical device — or when the task is medical imaging, where MedGemma has been specifically tuned.

Then, whichever you pick: define your evaluation before you tune anything, and expect to spend most of your time in the adapt-and-measure loop. Both worked examples in this session got the headline finding right and a supporting detail wrong. That gap is the work.
