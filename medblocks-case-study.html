The thesis

They are not selling access. They are selling the plumbing on top of access that already exists.

For twenty years, getting clinical data out of a hospital meant signing a contract with the hospital, or paying a broker who had. Both routes took months and cost a fortune. Medblocks’ founding observation is that this is no longer technically true: regulation now requires US health systems and payers to expose standardised FHIR APIs, and requires them to let a patient authorise any app they choose.

The docs state the position bluntly — “A great healthcare product that can’t integrate is just a demo.” Traditional vendors, they argue, function as access brokers: they own the relationship, they charge for it, and they lock you in. Medblocks instead hands you the machinery to walk through the doors regulation already opened, using your own credentials and your own brand. They claim this comes out roughly 10–12× cheaper than a bundled integration deal.

Three consequences fall out of that single decision, and they explain nearly every design choice in the product:

  • No sales cycle gates the first integration. If your product has a relationship with the patient, the patient can authorise you today. No hospital signature required. The docs call this “the fastest way to launch.”
  • They never hold the credentials. You register your app with Epic; you own the OAuth client. The patient approves your app on the consent screen. Medblocks is infrastructure behind it, not a party to it.
  • Their real product is everything after the token. Anyone can do one OAuth dance. The hard, unglamorous, permanent work is refreshing tokens forever, crawling paginated FHIR for years, reconciling the same patient across five systems, and making a lab value mean the same thing regardless of who sent it.

The uncomfortable part they lead with

Most integration vendors treat data reconciliation as a data-quality footnote. Medblocks frames it as a clinical safety problem: duplicate patient records and inconsistent clinical coding can harm a patient directly. That framing is why normalisation and master data management sit in the middle of their architecture rather than bolted on at the edge.

Mechanism · 01

Where the platform sits, and what it absorbs

Medblocks is middleware. It sits between your product and every system that holds patient data, and it draws a very specific line about what stays yours. Read the diagram as a boundary negotiation: everything in the middle column is work you are choosing not to do.

The docs reduce this to two nouns you will see on every page, so it is worth fixing them now:

NounDefinition from the docsIn practice
Source A catalog entry for a system Medblocks has already integrated — Epic, Oracle Health, athenahealth, eClinicalWorks, Meditech, CMS Blue Button, payer APIs, networks. Exists before anyone authorises anything. Carries display name, logo, portal URL, FHIR endpoint, vendor type, setup requirements.
Connection The live, authorised link between your app and one source. “A connection exists after authorization.” One per relationship — not one enterprise contract covering everything. Has a lifecycle you must handle.

And the pipeline is five stages, in this order: identify a source you can legitimately reach → establish a connection once authorised → pull records into the platform → monitor via webhooks and dashboard → export onward.

Mechanism · 02

Four doors into healthcare, and who holds each key

This is the most useful idea in the whole documentation set, and it is almost entirely non-technical. There are four legitimate ways to reach healthcare data, they differ by who can authorise you, and choosing the wrong one is how integration projects die. Medblocks positions itself as the only vendor spanning all four plus the data layer beneath them.

Door 1 · the patient authorises

Patient Access

The patient signs into their own portal — MyChart and its equivalents — and approves your app. This unlocks their EHR records, insurance data, and other patient-controlled sources.

Protocol
SMART on FHIR, OAuth 2.0 authorization code
Gate
You have a direct relationship with the patient
Speed
Fastest — no hospital contract at all

Door 2 · the provider org enables you

Clinician Workflow

Your app appears inside the EHR. SMART App Launch opens your full application with patient and user context pre-loaded; CDS Hooks returns cards at moments like opening a chart or placing an order.

Protocol
SMART App Launch · CDS Hooks discovery
Gate
An EHR administrator registers you
Why
“About usage, not just data” — puts you where the work happens

Door 3 · the org authorises your server

Backend

Server-to-server movement with no human in the loop. Scheduled, organisation-scoped syncs for cohort analysis, operational reporting and continuous background updates.

Protocol
SMART Backend Services · FHIR Bulk Data
Gate
Organisation-level authorisation
Shape
Batch, not interactive

Door 4 · a treatment relationship unlocks it

Network

Reach records from organisations you have no direct integration with, via TEFCA and CMS-aligned exchange networks. The docs are explicit that this “requires clinical context and permitted purpose” and is not a workaround for the relationship requirement.

Protocol
TEFCA / QHIN exchange
Gate

Care coordination across networks

Why this matters more than the code

Every technical decision downstream — which OAuth flow, whose credentials, what scopes, which legal agreement — is determined by which door you walked through. If you are replicating this, pick one door and finish it before opening a second. The four are not variations on a theme; they are four different products sharing a storage layer.

Mechanism · 03

The handshake, step by step

Door 1 is the one the docs teach first, so it is the one to understand in full. Below is the complete round trip, from a patient tapping a button to your server reading FHIR resources. Note where the work is asynchronous — this is the single most common thing teams get wrong.

What that looks like in code

The whole flow is four SDK calls. This is verbatim from their quickstart, trimmed to the essential path.

Install and initialise · src/medblocks.ts

// npm install medblocks   (pnpm / yarn / bun all supported)
// .env →  MEDBLOCKS_API_KEY="mb_sk_live_..."

import { Medblocks } from "medblocks";

const apiKey = process.env.MEDBLOCKS_API_KEY;
if (!apiKey) throw new Error("MEDBLOCKS_API_KEY is not configured");

export const mb = new Medblocks(apiKey);

Step 1 — send the patient to the hosted flow

const session = await mb.patientSession.init({
  patient_id: "demo-patient",                      // YOUR id for this person
  return_url: "https://app.example.com/connected", // must be HTTPS in prod
  return_button_label: "Back to Acme Health",      // your brand, not theirs
});

res.redirect(session.url);

Step 2 — handle the return, then verify server-side

import { parseReturnUrl } from "medblocks";

const result = parseReturnUrl(new URL(req.url, origin).searchParams);
if (!result) return res.status(400).send("Not a return URL");

// Treat the return URL as UI state only. Confirm with a server-side read.
const connections = await mb.patients.getConnections(result.patient_id, {
  hydrate: true,
});
const facilities = connections.map((c) => c.name).join(", ");

Step 3 — read the records once the pull lands

const page = await mb.patients.records(patientId, { count: 100 });
// → GET /patients/{id}/records — paginated list of latest stored resources

for (const item of page.data) {
  console.log(item.resource.resourceType, item.resource.id);
}

That is the entire integration. The remaining 95% of the documentation is about the parts that go wrong afterwards — which is exactly where the real engineering is.

Mechanism · 04

The data model, and the state machine you must respect

A connection is not a boolean. It is a long-lived credential that decays, and the docs give a firm instruction about it: “Your app should act on connection state, not just on whether a browser flow finished.” Here are the states they define and what each one obliges you to do.

StateMeaningWhat your app must do
activeAccess functions and records flow normally.Nothing. Show the facility as connected.
failedAuthorization did not complete successfully.Offer a retry. Never show a half-connected state.
refresh_failedPreviously working access; token refresh now fails.Prompt a reconnect. This is the state most teams forget.
expiredAccess is no longer usable.Treat as disconnected; stop promising fresh data.
disconnectedAccess was revoked for your workspace — by you or by the patient.Honour it immediately. Consider your retention policy.

Whose credentials, and why it matters

Medblocks runs a bring-your-own-credentials model. You can start on Medblocks-supplied credentials for testing, then switch a connection to your own when you are ready to launch. The docs are unambiguous about the reason: “The access is tied to your app, your customer relationship, and your compliance workflow.” Patients and clinicians approve your registered app, not a generic intermediary — which is both a branding win and the thing that prevents lock-in.

Mechanism · 05

FHIR is a transport format, not an answer

This is where Medblocks earns most of its money, and where a replication attempt is most likely to stall. Their framing: “FHIR is the transport format, but source data still varies.” Every system speaks FHIR and no two say the same thing the same way.

Consider a question as trivial as “what is this patient’s latest BMI?” Across five hospitals you may find it as a calculated Observation, as height and weight you must compute from, under different LOINC codes, in metric or imperial units, duplicated three times from the same encounter, or buried in a PDF document. Raw FHIR does not resolve any of that. Clinical Domain Models are Medblocks’ answer: product-facing abstractions layered over the raw store.

The docs list four jobs the domain models do:

  1. Clinical abstraction — medications, labs, conditions, allergies, visits and documents as meaningful entities, not raw resources.
  2. Normalised querying — “common filters and display fields are lifted into a consistent shape,” so one query works regardless of origin.
  3. Source preservation — they keep “source context you need for auditability.”
  4. Provenance — resource references, timestamps and performer details, so downstream audit trails hold up.

Underneath sit the unglamorous mechanics: unit conversion (BMI in kg/m²), code-system mapping (LOINC 39156-5 for body mass index), and aggregation rules — latest, average, minimum — which is also where de-duplication of repeated observations from one encounter gets resolved.

Illustrative shape of a normalised value — not a verbatim doc sample

  "code":   { "system": "http://loinc.org", "code": "39156-5",
              "display": "Body mass index (BMI) [Ratio]" },
  "value":  27.4,
  "unit":   "kg/m2",
  "effective": "2026-08-14T09:12:00Z",

    "source":   "epic-sandbox",
    "resource": "Observation/eXyZ-1024",   // trace back to raw
    "performer":"Practitioner/9a2f"
  }

Reconciliation is two problems, not one

Identity: is the Jane Smith from Hospital A the same Jane Smith from Hospital B? That is master data management, and it is statistical, never certain.

Meaning: does “metformin 500mg” from one system refer to the same drug concept as another system’s coding? That is terminology mapping — LOINC for labs, RxNorm for drugs, SNOMED CT for clinical findings.

Medblocks resolves both before data reaches your application. If you replicate this, budget for both separately; they need different skills and different people.

Mechanism · 06

Three exits, chosen by what you are feeding

Once records are ingested there are exactly three ways out, and the docs are clear about which to use when.

ExitShapeUse it when
Exports FHIR R4 resources pushed to a FHIR server, or NDJSON files written to S3-compatible storage, GCS, or Azure Blob. Initial full export, then deltas. You are keeping a downstream system in sync — a warehouse, a lakehouse, a customer’s own FHIR endpoint.
Webhooks Signed POST events. Four of them. Something in your product must react — refresh a dashboard, page an on-call engineer, prompt a reconnect.
API / SDK reads GET /patients/{id}/records, paginated. Operational queries and on-demand inspection of one patient.

The four events

These are the complete set, and they map neatly onto the failure modes above.

EventFires whenHandle it by
patient_session.completedPatient finishes the hosted flow.Update UI, kick off downstream work.
records.sync.completedA background pull finishes.Refresh views, process the new data.
records.sync.failedA pull fails.Alert on-call.
connection.token_refresh_failedA refresh token is no longer usable.Ask the patient to reconnect.

The receiver contract is strict and worth copying verbatim into your own platform if you build one: accept HTTPS POST with JSON, read the raw body before parsing, validate the Medblocks-Signature header against the endpoint’s signing secret, respond 2xx within 5 seconds, and de-duplicate on event.id because deliveries can repeat.

Webhook receiver — the signature check is not optional

// Raw body FIRST. Parsed JSON will not verify.
const raw = await readRawBody(req);
const sig = req.headers["medblocks-signature"];

const event = mb.webhooks.constructEvent(raw, sig, process.env.MB_WEBHOOK_SECRET);

if (await alreadyHandled(event.id)) return res.status(200).end();  // idempotent

switch (event.type) {
  case "records.sync.completed":          await reindexPatient(event); break;
  case "records.sync.failed":             await pageOnCall(event);     break;
  case "connection.token_refresh_failed": await promptReconnect(event); break;
  case "patient_session.completed":       await markConnected(event);  break;
}
res.status(200).end();  // under 5 seconds — queue the real work

How they make exports joinable

A detail worth stealing: exported Patient resources carry your patient identifier inside the FHIR identifier array, alongside the source EHR’s own identifiers such as MRNs. In object storage the matching key is the identifier system urn:medblocks:patient-id. That single convention is what lets a warehouse join hospital data back to your application’s users without a separate mapping table.

Other operational properties of the export layer: each destination runs independently, so one failure does not block the others; disabled destinations are skipped and resume from their checkpoint when re-enabled; NDJSON files are rewritten in full on each run — which means your ingestion must be idempotent; and watermark tracking drives delta detection.

Mechanism · 07

The half of the product that only exists because things break

Skim the documentation tree and a pattern emerges: a small number of pages teach the happy path, and a large number exist purely to answer “it didn’t work, why?” This is the most transferable lesson in the whole case study.

Pull monitoring — did the records arrive?

A pull is the background job that retrieves records after authorisation. It starts when a session yields an active connection, when an admin triggers it manually, or on a schedule or retry. Internally it runs FHIR queries per supported resource type, fetches resources, follows references, stores the data, and logs status throughout — refreshing access tokens proactively before expiry where possible. The dashboard shows per-patient record status, last pull time, account-level status and full history. Crucially: pull success and export success are independent. A connection can be healthy while its exports are failing.

Auth event history — did the patient even reach the portal?

A separate audit trail for the authorisation attempt itself, covering the whole OAuth flow from portal redirect through token refresh. It answers the two questions support actually gets asked: did the patient reach the portal? and did the token exchange succeed? Each event records source name, status, a stable error code, a human-readable description, and a timestamp. The docs ask you to log session_id, connection_id, success, error, error_description and the Medblocks request_id on your side so the two trails line up.

A stable error envelope

Every non-2xx response has the same shape, and every code is documented. This is table stakes for a platform whose users are debugging someone else’s hospital at 2am.

{
  "error": {
    "type":       "string",
    "code":       "string",   // stable — safe to switch on
    "message":    "string",
    "param":      "string or null",
    "doc_url":    "string",
    "request_id": "string"    // quote this to support
  }
}

Sandboxes that are actually usable

Sandbox and production are separate workspaces with separate keys — mb_sk_sbx_ and mb_sk_live_ — and a sandbox key cannot open a session against a production source, or vice versa. Sandbox workspaces get their own sources, webhooks, exports and dashboard history. Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, eClinicalWorks, Meditech and CMS Blue Button all expose sandbox portals whose behaviour is “close enough to test OAuth and SMART on FHIR.” You can load 10 pre-generated synthetic patients or upload your own FHIR bundles. The honest caveat is in the docs too: synthetic records are good for UI states, mapping logic, exports and webhook handlers, and useless for judging real source coverage or data quality.

An MCP server, because the integrator is now an agent

A genuinely forward-looking piece: the platform exposes an MCP server at https://app.medblocks.com/mcp (server name medblocks-platform) with around ten tools — workspace overview, health-system search, connection management, record reads, and deletion. OAuth for interactive use, API keys for backend services.

# Claude Code
claude mcp add --transport http medblocks https://app.medblocks.com/mcp

# with a workspace API key instead of interactive OAuth
claude mcp add --transport http medblocks https://app.medblocks.com/mcp \
  --header "Authorization: Bearer mb_sk_..."

# Codex

  --oauth-resource https://app.medblocks.com/mcp

They also ship installable skills for Claude Code, Cursor and Codex that teach an agent the patient access flow, record export, and the security rules — summarised as five non-negotiables: keys server-side only, verify webhook signatures before trusting a payload, treat the API reference as authoritative for wire shapes, log error codes and request ids server-side, and treat patient return URLs as UI state confirmed by a server-side read.

Security posture, stated plainly

HIPAA, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001 and GDPR, with BAA support on commercial plans. Their go-live checklist is the useful artefact: BAA executed, production credentials configured, webhook receivers verifying Medblocks-Signature, export destinations owned by your organisation, access controls and audit logging and retention policies implemented in your app, and application logs that exclude raw PHI unless explicitly permitted. They are explicit that they sit “in the path of PHI, patient authorization, source credentials, export credentials” — and that deciding what to retain is your job, not theirs.

The economics

Free until it works, then a flat number

The pricing is unusually legible for health IT, and the metering choice is the interesting part.

PlanPriceCapacityIncludes
Free $0 1,000 active patients Patient-mediated exchange, API access, device sync, exports
Enterprise $60,000 / year Unlimited Everything in Free, plus Backend Services, Clinical Workflows, OCR, data structuring

A patient counts as active the first time data is successfully pulled for them — creating a record costs nothing. That single definition means you pay only for integrations that actually worked, which removes the main financial risk of experimenting. Hit the cap and new sync attempts return 402 with quota_exceeded; ask for a feature outside your plan and you get feature_not_enabled instead.

Note what is behind the paywall: Patient Access — Door 1 — is free. Doors 2 and 3, and the data-structuring layer, are not. That is a deliberate funnel: give away the door that needs no contract, charge for the ones that come with enterprise customers attached.

Replication · the blueprint

If you want to build this, here is the shape of the work

Start by looking honestly at what you are choosing between. The diagram below is the same eight-layer stack twice: once where six layers are purchased, once where they are not.

If you are going ahead, the sequence below is the one that works. It is ordered by dependency, not by difficulty — each phase is unblocked by the one before it, and every phase ends in something demonstrable.

Phase 04–12 weeks, mostly waiting

Legal and registration, started before any code

This is the real critical path and it runs in parallel with everything else. Begin it on day one.

  • Register a developer account and an app with your first vendor. Epic’s developer programme, Oracle Health’s, athenahealth’s — each has its own review. Expect weeks, not days, and expect to declare your redirect URIs and requested scopes up front.
  • Decide your PHI posture now: where data lives, how long you keep it, who can read it. It determines your schema.
  • Start SOC 2 Type II. The observation window alone is months; a compliance automation platform shortens the paperwork, not the clock.
  • Line up BAAs with every subprocessor that will touch PHI — cloud, logging, error tracking, queue.

Phase 14–8 weeks

One source, one patient, end to end

Resist breadth. Get a single sandbox — Epic’s is the obvious choice — all the way from consent screen to stored resource.

  • Implement SMART on FHIR authorization code flow with PKCE. Discover endpoints from the server’s .well-known/smart-configuration rather than hard-coding them.
  • Build the hosted consent flow: a facility picker, a consent screen, a redirect back to a caller-supplied return_url. Your branding, your domain.
  • Store the token pair encrypted. Envelope encryption with a managed KMS key, never plaintext, never in application logs.
  • Write a single-shot pull: query the supported resource types, page through Bundle.link[next], follow references, persist raw.

Done when: a synthetic patient in the Epic sandbox authorises your app and their resources land in your database.

Phase 26–10 weeks

Durability — the phase that decides whether this is a product

Phase 1 is a demo. This is where it becomes something you can run. Every item here maps to a specific Medblocks page, which tells you they all turned out to be necessary.

  • Connection state machine. Model all five states explicitly. Expose the state, not a boolean.
  • Proactive token refresh. A scheduled job that refreshes before expiry. Refresh tokens rotate on many vendors — persist the new one atomically or you will lock yourself out.
  • Durable pull orchestration. Pulls are long-running, resumable, and must survive a deploy mid-crawl. This is a workflow-engine problem, not a cron problem.
  • Signed webhook delivery with retries, an event id for idempotency, and a replay tool.
  • Two audit trails, separately. One for authorisation attempts, one for pull runs. Medblocks split these because support questions split that way: did they reach the portal? versus did the records arrive?
  • A stable error envelope with codes you commit to, and a request id in every response.

Phase 33–6 months, then forever

Meaning — identity, then terminology

Two distinct projects. Do identity first; normalisation on top of duplicate patients produces confidently wrong answers.

  • Patient MDM. Start deterministic — name, date of birth, sex, address, MRN — then add probabilistic matching. Keep a human review queue for the middle band; in healthcare a false merge is worse than a missed one.
  • Terminology. Map to LOINC (labs), RxNorm (medications), SNOMED CT (findings). Stand up a terminology server rather than embedding code tables in application logic.
  • Units and aggregation. UCUM conversion, plus explicit rules for latest / average / minimum.
  • Two layers, always. Keep the raw store immutable and derive the normalised layer from it, with provenance on every field pointing back. This is what makes you auditable, and it is the only way to re-derive when a mapping turns out to be wrong.

Phase 44–6 weeks

Fan-out

Comparatively ordinary engineering, and the phase where borrowing their design decisions pays off directly.

  • FHIR R4 push with no-auth, bearer, and OAuth 2.0 client-credentials options; validate connectivity before saving a destination.
  • NDJSON to S3-compatible, GCS and Azure Blob — one resource per line so consumers can stream.
  • Watermarks per destination for delta detection; independent failure domains; resume from checkpoint on re-enable.
  • Copy the identifier convention. Stamp your own patient id into the exported Patient.identifier array under a namespaced system. It is the difference between a joinable warehouse and a useless one.

Phase 5per surface

Additional doors, only once Door 1 is boring

  • More vendors on Door 1: 2–4 weeks each, and most of that is registration latency rather than code. Endpoint directories are published — the ONC-maintained public FHIR endpoint lists are where a facility picker gets its catalogue.
  • Door 2, clinician workflow: implement SMART App Launch (EHR-initiated launch with patient context) and a CDS Hooks discovery endpoint serving cards for hooks like patient-view and order-select. Requires an EHR admin to register you per organisation.
  • Door 3, backend: SMART Backend Services — asymmetric client authentication with a JWK set — plus FHIR Bulk Data async export polling.
  • Door 4, network: you do not build this. TEFCA participation means joining through a QHIN and satisfying permitted-purpose requirements. Treat it as a legal and partnership workstream with a thin technical component.

What to actually use for each layer

Replication · the honest part

Five things that will cost more than you planned

Not risks in the abstract — these are the specific places this class of project reliably overruns.

1 · Vendor registration is a calendar problem, not an engineering one

Your first production Epic app registration can take weeks to months, and every vendor has its own process, its own scope vocabulary, and its own review. No amount of engineering velocity compresses it. Start all of them in parallel on day one, and never let a launch date depend on an approval you do not control.

2 · Token refresh at scale is a quiet, expensive failure mode

One connection refreshing is trivial. Ten thousand connections across a dozen vendors, each with its own expiry semantics and some rotating the refresh token on every use, is a distributed systems problem with a nasty property: failures are silent and permanent. A dropped rotation locks that patient out until they re-authorise, and you will not find out until they complain. The fact that Medblocks gives refresh_failed its own connection state and its own webhook tells you how often this fires in production.

3 · Every vendor’s FHIR is a dialect

All of them claim R4. In practice they differ on which resources exist, which search parameters work, how deep _include goes, page sizes, rate limits, date handling, and what an error body looks like. The Medblocks docs concede the point about their own sandboxes — behaviour is “close enough to test OAuth and SMART on FHIR,” with production quirks that differ. Budget per-vendor work indefinitely; this line item never goes to zero.

4 · Normalisation is an ongoing obligation, not a project

LOINC and RxNorm ship releases. Vendors change how they code things. A mapping you got wrong two years ago is sitting in a customer’s warehouse right now. The only sane architecture is the one Medblocks uses: immutable raw layer, derived normalised layer, provenance on every field — so that when a mapping is wrong you can re-derive rather than apologise.

5 · The unit economics are brutal at small scale

Enterprise access is $60,000/year with unlimited patients, and the first 1,000 patients cost nothing. Three engineers for a year costs several times that before you have shipped Phase 3. The economics only invert if healthcare data integration is your product — if you are selling it, or if it is the thing that differentiates you and you cannot accept a dependency in the middle of it.

Verdict

Copy the architecture. Buy the integrations.

Medblocks’ documentation is worth reading even if you never sign up, because it is a compressed field guide to a domain that punishes naïve designs. Six of its decisions are worth adopting regardless of what you build on:

  • Separate the four doors explicitly. Who authorises you determines everything downstream. Do not build one abstraction over all four.
  • Connections are stateful, and state is public API. Five states, exposed, with an event for the one that decays silently.
  • Authorisation finishing ≠ data arriving. Two separate audit trails, because support questions split exactly there.
  • Two storage layers. Immutable raw for audit, derived normalised for product, provenance linking them.
  • Reconciliation is clinical safety. Identity and meaning are different problems, both resolved before data reaches an application.
  • Stamp your own identifier into every export. One convention, and your warehouse joins.

So: build or adopt?

Adopt if healthcare data is an input to your product. The free tier covers 1,000 active patients and a patient only counts once data has actually been pulled, so the entire Door 1 integration can be validated at zero cost — which is less than one week of one engineer. Spend your year on whatever your product does with the data instead.

Build only in three situations: healthcare data integration is the product you are selling; you need a surface, geography or source no vendor covers; or a regulatory or contractual constraint genuinely forbids PHI transiting a third party. In the first case you are choosing to compete, and should read their comparisons page as a feature checklist. In the second, build only the missing door and buy the rest.

The hybrid nobody advertises is usually right: adopt for acquisition — the doors, the tokens, the crawl — and own the normalisation and storage layers yourself, where your clinical logic actually lives and where you least want a black box. Their export-to-your-own-FHIR-server path exists precisely to make that shape possible.
