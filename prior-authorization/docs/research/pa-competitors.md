# Who's Using PA Automation and How They're Performing

*Prepared for a CTO audience. Last researched: September 2026.*

## What is Prior Authorization, and why does it matter to a technologist?

**Prior Authorization (PA)** is the approval a doctor's office must get from a patient's health insurer ("**payer**" — the company or health plan that pays medical claims) before certain tests, procedures, surgeries, or drugs are covered. If the provider skips this step, the payer can refuse to pay, leaving the patient or provider on the hook.

Today PA is mostly manual: phone calls, faxes, and web portals, with clinical staff spending hours per request compiling records for a human reviewer at the insurer. Industry estimates put the physician time cost at 43 PA requests handled per physician per week, and each PA "episode" costs payers roughly $40–$50 and providers $20–$30 to process (Peterson Health Technology Institute, 2026). This is the reason PA automation has become one of the hottest applied-AI categories in healthcare — and one of the most fraught, since a bad automated decision can delay someone's cancer treatment.

Two forces are converging on this market right now:
1. **A federal deadline.** The CMS-0057-F rule requires most Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and ACA marketplace payers to expose **FHIR** (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources — the standard format health systems use to exchange data electronically) **APIs for prior authorization by January 1, 2027.**
2. **A public pledge.** In July 2025, 48 insurers (coordinated by the trade group AHIP, covering ~257 million Americans) voluntarily committed to real-time decisions, human clinician review of denials, and standardized electronic PA — under political pressure following high-profile controversies (see the UnitedHealth/nH Predict case below).

This document profiles the real organizations building, buying, or selling PA automation, what they claim, and how credible those claims are.

## How to read the numbers in this document

Every performance figure below is labeled:
- **[Vendor claim]** — self-reported by the company in a press release, blog, or sales material. Not independently audited. Treat as a best case / marketing number.
- **[Customer case study]** — a named health system or payer reporting results from a real deployment, usually co-published with the vendor. More credible than a vendor's own number, but still not independently audited, and usually the best-case customer the vendor chose to feature.
- **[Independent/third-party]** — from a source with no commercial stake (a regulator, academic study, journalist investigation, or a research nonprofit like PHTI). This is the most reliable category and, tellingly, the smallest.

No source in this space is a randomized controlled trial. Where a company gave us no public numbers at all, we say so explicitly rather than guess.

**What is "gold-carding"?** A payer exempts a provider from needing PA at all for certain services, once that provider has a track record of high approval rates (commonly ≥90% approval on past requests for that service). It's a policy lever, not a technology — but it's often paired with AI-driven tracking of provider performance to decide who qualifies.

**Independent numbers on the industry-wide pledge**: As of the one-year mark, AHIP reported insurers had eliminated 6.5 million prior authorizations — an 11% reduction — since the July 2025 pledge. This is an AHIP-published (industry association) figure, not independently audited, and outside observers (KFF Health News, physician groups) have expressed skepticism about how much has actually changed for patients.

## 5. The independent counter-narrative: does any of this actually work?

Two credible, non-vendor sources complicate the industry's success narrative — a CTO evaluating a build/buy decision should weigh these heavily:

- **Peterson Health Technology Institute (PHTI), April 2026 report — "Administrative AI: Current Use and Potential Impact."** **[Independent/third-party]** PHTI (a nonprofit, no vendor stake) found that while AI reduces manual effort *within* individual organizations, it has **not lowered system-wide costs** and may be increasing them — because faster PA submission on one side triggers more back-and-forth, more complexity, and more automated pushback on the other side (an "AI arms race" between provider-side submission AI and payer-side review/denial AI). Each PA cycle still costs payers ~$40–$50 and providers ~$20–$30, and PHTI found no evidence yet that AI reduces the all-in cost per claim once the cost of the AI tooling itself is included.
- **The UnitedHealth / nH Predict litigation.** **[Independent/third-party]** This is the sector's starkest evidence that "AI speeds things up" and "AI makes good decisions" are different claims. A Senate investigation and ongoing class-action litigation allege a ~90% appeal-reversal rate on an algorithm used to inform post-acute-care coverage decisions, and found the payer's denial rate roughly doubled after the tool's introduction. As of 2026 the litigation is ongoing and UnitedHealth has been ordered to disclose more about how the tool functions.

**Takeaway for a CTO:** Vendor-reported metrics in this document (turnaround time, first-pass approval rate, touchless-processing percentage) are consistently impressive and consistently self-reported or co-published with a hand-picked customer. The two genuinely independent data points in this space — PHTI and the nH Predict litigation — both suggest caution: speed and automation don't automatically equal lower system cost or fairer decisions, and a legally and clinically defensible audit trail matters as much as raw throughput.
