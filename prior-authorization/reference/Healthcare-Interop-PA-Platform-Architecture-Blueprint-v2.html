2 Executive Summary

We propose to build a healthcare interoperability platform with capabilities comparable to Medblocks-style data-access platforms: connecting to EHRs, normalizing clinical data, and notifying downstream systems. On top of it we would add a prior authorization (PA) engine that carries a request from the moment a clinician signs an order to the moment the payer's decision is written back into the EHR.

1. What are we building?

A multi-tenant, API-first platform in two layers Recommended:

  • Interoperability layer: EHR and payer connections, SMART on FHIR, a FHIR R4 data platform, terminology, documents, events and webhooks, and audit.
  • Prior authorization layer: a case engine that orchestrates the Da Vinci CRD → DTR → PAS workflow, with managed fallbacks (X12 278 via a clearinghouse, or a tracked portal task) for payers that are not ready.
2–3. Why does it exist, and what problem does it solve?

Prior authorization is still largely manual: phone, fax, and payer portals. CMS estimates that requesting prior authorizations takes providers an average of 13 hours per week, about $34,000 a year per provider[25] Verified.

From 1 January 2027, CMS-regulated payers must operate a FHIR Prior Authorization API[24] Verified. Providers still need someone to connect their EHR workflows to many payers that implement those APIs unevenly.

4. How is it related to Medblocks?

Medblocks publicly documents a data-integration platform. Its core concepts are sources, connections, pulls, exports, webhooks, clinician-workflow embedding via SMART and CDS Hooks, and clinical domain models[2,3,4] Verified.

Its public documentation does not describe a prior authorization product. Its September 2026 PA article is educational[21] Verified.

We adopt the functional pattern and design our own implementation, then add the PA layer. The two are not affiliated.

5. How does it enable prior authorization?
  • It pulls the patient's coverage and clinical evidence from the EHR (SMART / US Core).
  • It discovers payer requirements (CRD) and renders the payer's documentation template with pre-filled answers (DTR).
  • It submits a standards-based request (PAS Claim/$submit) and tracks pended responses through subscriptions.
  • It routes requests for more information back to staff, then writes the decision and authorization number back to the EHR Recommended.
6. Who uses it?
  • Primary users: provider PA and revenue-cycle staff, and ordering clinicians (inside the EHR).
  • Customers: provider groups and health systems, health-tech and RCM companies that embed our APIs, and prior-authorization vendors.
  • Later: patients (status visibility) and payers (§9) Recommended.
7. What are the major components?

API gateway and identity; tenant and connection management; a pluggable connector framework (EHR, payer, clearinghouse, HIE); a FHIR repository (HAPI FHIR recommended); terminology service; document service; PA case engine with workflow orchestration; DTR app; PAS client; events and webhooks; audit; admin and developer portals (§10) Recommended.

8. What standards are required?
  • Core: HL7 FHIR R4 (4.0.1), US Core, SMART App Launch 2.x (including Backend Services), OAuth 2.0 / OIDC, CDS Hooks.
  • Da Vinci: CRD 2.2.1, DTR 2.2.0 and PAS 2.2.1 are the current published versions. CMS lists earlier versions as recommended, and ONC approved these for voluntary certification use from 29 Aug 2026[48,51,53,64] Verified.
  • Supporting: Subscriptions R5 Backport, CDex, and X12 278/275 for fallback.
  • Code sets: SNOMED CT, LOINC, RxNorm, ICD-10-CM, CPT/HCPCS, NDC.
9. What is the MVP?

Medical-benefit PA only, with Recommended:

  • Epic plus one other EHR, via SMART launch and US Core reads;
  • one payer with a FHIR PA API (CRD/DTR/PAS) and one clearinghouse X12 278 route;
  • our DTR app, PAS client, and case engine with work queue and SLA timers;
  • documents, webhooks, audit, and an admin console.

Every other payer is a tracked manual task, with no silent gaps (§28).

10. What are the major risks?
  1. Payer readiness varies. UnitedHealthcare labels its FHIR PA API "coming soon" and Blue Cross NC says "coming 2027", while trade press reports Epic's CRD live with three payers at four health systems[83,84,116].
  2. EHR onboarding lead times. EHR app registration, customer activation, and production access take time.
  3. EHR-native competition. EHR vendors are building native CRD/DTR/PAS.
  4. Rules are still moving. CMS-0062-P would change the HIPAA standards for PA, but it is only proposed[26,27] Proposed rule.
  5. PHI security and HIPAA minimum-necessary obligations. PA disclosures are "payment" activities, so the treatment exception does not apply[93,94].
  6. Data quality and terminology gaps that break automated pre-population.

The full register is in §31.

3 Business Problem

3.1 Prior authorization in plain terms

In plain terms

Before a health plan pays for certain treatments, such as an MRI, a knee arthroscopy, a specialty infusion, or a power wheelchair, it asks the doctor's office to prove the treatment is covered and medically necessary. That approval is called prior authorization (also "pre-authorization", "pre-certification", or "prior approval").

Each plan has its own list of services that need approval, its own forms and clinical criteria, and its own way of receiving requests. Office staff spend hours finding out whether approval is needed, gathering chart notes, filling in forms, faxing or uploading them, and chasing status. Patients wait in the meantime.

3.3 The gap this platform fills

The mandate falls on payers. Providers still face these gaps Inferred:

  1. Many payers, uneven APIs. Each payer's endpoints, onboarding, trust registration, and supported versions differ. Some payers will support only part of CRD/DTR/PAS for some time.
  2. Many EHRs, uneven capability. Some EHRs ship native CRD/DTR/PAS. Others will rely on third-party SMART apps or none at all. Practices on smaller EHRs are the least served.
  3. Operations, not just transactions. Staff need work queues, deadline tracking, requests for more information, appeals, and reporting across all payers, including the ones still on portals and fax.
  4. Clinical data quality. Automated questionnaire pre-population only works when coded data (diagnoses, labs, prior therapies) is present and normalized.

5.1 What it is

Medblocks describes itself as "one integration for all healthcare data." It connects an application to EHRs, networks, patient portals and clinician workflows[1] Verified. Its documentation says it is "built for product teams that need healthcare data access without becoming integration infrastructure teams"[1] Verified.

The current product documentation centers on a product Medblocks calls Medblocks Connect in its billing documentation[18] Verified. Separately, Medblocks has earlier "Medblocks Stack" / Digital Health Platform material describing an openEHR + FHIR + SMART + S3 architecture, and it originated the openFHIR mapping engine[20,23] Verified. These are different offerings. The earlier stack material should not be read as a description of how Connect is built Inferred.

5.2 The problem it solves

7 Lessons from Medblocks

What we should copy (as patterns), what we must add, and where a PA platform differs fundamentally from a data-access platform.

Where PA differs from a data-access platform

  • Bidirectional and stateful. Data access is mostly read-and-deliver. PA is a long-running, multi-party case: states, deadlines, requests for more information, appeals, and write-back into the EHR Industry-standard.
  • Payer counterparties. Each payer is a separately onboarded API partner, with trust registration (for example, DTR requires payers to authenticate DTR apps via SMART Backend Services)[52] Verified.
  • Latency budgets. CRD servers must respond within 5 seconds (10 seconds for certain hooks) 90% of the time[50]. PAS aims for a response within 15 seconds or returns a pended response[54] Verified. Background-pull architectures cannot meet these on their own. A synchronous path is required.
  • Minimum necessary. Delivering "all records" is appropriate for many data-access use cases. A payer PA disclosure must be limited to what is needed for the purpose[94] Verified.
  • Accountability for correctness. Wrong data in a dashboard is a defect. A wrong "no PA needed" becomes a denied claim and a patient bill Inferred.

8 Proposed Platform Vision

Working name: Healthcare Interoperability & Prior Authorization Platform ("the Platform"). The name is temporary and not final.

Vision statement Recommended

Every order that needs payer approval is detected at the moment it is placed. It is documented once, from data already in the chart. It is submitted through the best available standard channel, tracked to a decision without anyone phoning a payer, and closed back in the EHR with a complete audit trail.

15.1 Why terminology normalization matters for PA

In plain terms

Payer rules are written in standard codes: "CPT 72148 requires PA when diagnosis is in this ICD-10 list and conservative therapy of at least 6 weeks is documented." If the chart stores the diagnosis under a local code, stores the therapy as free text, or files the lab without a LOINC code, the automation cannot "see" the evidence. The questionnaire then comes back blank and a person has to hunt for it. Normalization is the difference between a form that fills itself and a form that doesn't.

  • CQL pre-population in DTR evaluates FHIR data against ValueSets. Membership depends on standard codes[52] Verified.
  • PAS intermediaries may need "terminology translations (e.g., SNOMED to ICD10)" when mapping to X12[54] Verified.
  • Payer policies are keyed on CPT/HCPCS service codes and ICD-10-CM diagnoses Industry-standard.

16 Prior Authorization Business Workflow

This section explains prior authorization first for a business reader (Level 2), then maps each step to who is responsible. The technical contract for each step is in §18.

16.1 The story of one authorization

In plain terms: a lumbar MRI
  1. A patient with six weeks of back pain and leg numbness sees an orthopedic clinician, who orders a lumbar spine MRI.
  2. The EHR knows the patient's insurance. The payer's rules say this MRI needs approval for this plan, and the payer wants to know about symptom duration, conservative therapy tried, and neurological findings.
  3. Most of that is already in the chart: visit notes, a physical-therapy referral, medication history. A form pre-fills itself, and the clinician confirms two answers the chart didn't contain.
  4. The request goes electronically to the payer. Seconds later the answer is either "approved, authorization number X, valid for 60 days" or "pended, under clinical review".
  5. If pended, the payer may ask for the physical-therapy notes. Staff are alerted, attach them, and send. The decision arrives later and the EHR is updated automatically. The scheduler books the MRI.
  6. If denied, the denial carries a specific reason (required for CMS-regulated payers since 1 Jan 2026[24]). The clinician can request a peer-to-peer discussion or file an appeal.

16.4 Urgent vs. standard

CMS-0057-F impacted payers (excluding QHP issuers on FFEs) must decide within 72 hours for expedited (urgent) requests and 7 calendar days for standard requests[24] Verified. The Platform must capture urgency with a justification, set internal timers shorter than the regulatory limit, and escalate before breach Recommended. Payer-specific definitions of "urgent" and state-law timelines vary Needs validation.

16.5 Medical-service PA vs. pharmacy PA

  • Medical-benefit PA covers procedures, imaging, DME, admissions and drugs administered and billed under the medical benefit. It is the focus of CMS-0057-F's PA API, which excludes drugs[24]. CMS-0062-P would add medical-benefit drugs to the PA API Proposed rule[26].
  • Pharmacy-benefit PA covers retail and specialty prescriptions adjudicated by a PBM. It runs over NCPDP SCRIPT ePA transactions through ePA networks[87]. For Medicare Part D, NCPDP SCRIPT ePA transactions were adopted in a 2020 rule[37] Historical, and SCRIPT 2023011 (including ePA transactions) is required from 1 Jan 2028[36]. CMS-0062-P would require NCPDP standards for pharmacy-benefit drug PA across impacted payers Proposed rule[26]. The AHIP/BCBSA commitments exclude pharmacy benefits[117].
  • Implication. The two share the case engine, work queue, documents and audit. They need different connectors, contracts and certification. We recommend a separate product track after the medical MVP Recommended.

  1. Create the plugin from the template; declare the manifest (capabilities, auth methods, config schema).

  3. Add a mapping pack and golden fixtures (recorded sandbox responses, scrubbed).
  4. Pass the contract test suite (shared across all connectors) and the relevant Inferno kits.
  5. Release behind a feature flag per tenant; promote through readiness levels: lab → sandbox → pilot → production.

34 Conclusion

34.1 Final recommended target architecture

34.2 Summary

A Medblocks-like interoperability layer is necessary but not sufficient for prior authorization. Medblocks' public documentation shows a robust pattern for connecting to EHRs and payers, managing connection state, normalizing data with provenance, and delivering it through exports and signed webhooks. We should adopt that pattern[2,3,13]. Prior authorization adds four things:

  • a stateful, deadline-driven case engine;
  • payer counterparties with their own trust and version constraints;
  • synchronous latency budgets (CRD 5 s, PAS 15 s);
  • minimum-necessary disclosure obligations[50,54,94].

The regulatory timing (payer PA APIs from 1 Jan 2027[24]), the certification criteria (HTI-4[63]) and the voluntary industry commitments[117] make this the right window. Early live deployments exist[116]. Payer readiness is uneven[83,84], and PA attachment standards remain unsettled[31]. A product that guarantees a path for every case (FHIR where possible, partner X12 or tracked manual work where not) will be valuable throughout the transition.

34.3 Immediate next steps (next 90 days) Recommended

  1. Secure one design partner (Epic site) and one pilot payer with a FHIR PA test environment. Confirm IG versions.
  2. Enroll in EHR developer programs (open.epic; second EHR). Register sandbox clients. Build a SMART launch prototype.
  3. Stand up the Da Vinci reference payer and Inferno kits in a lab environment. Prototype a DTR render and a PAS submit.
  4. Complete the HIPAA risk analysis, policies, AWS BAA and a BAA template. Name the security and privacy officers.
  5. Approve ADRs 001–008 and the MVP scope (§28). Staff the core team (§30).
