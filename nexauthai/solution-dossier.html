The problem, sized honestly

The work as it is done today · what it costs · where the numbers come from

Prior authorization is the single most repetitive, phone-heavy administrative burden in a physician practice. A physician's practice spends 12–14 hours a week per physician on it, and 40% of practices hire staff exclusively to do it. The work itself is almost entirely the movement of data between systems that already hold it.

Honesty note 1 · the business case contradicts its own best source

The folder's ROI maths runs on $20–30 per manual PA, taken from a screenshot of a search result. Its own pricing workbook cites CAQH at $12.88. The “~5-day onboarding payback” claim is built on the higher figure. Re-derive anything client-facing on CAQH, or state the basis explicitly — and baseline against the practice's own data rather than either number.

Honesty note 2 · the one independent study disagrees with the market

The Peterson Health Technology Institute's 2026 report — the only non-vendor study in the source library — found that AI PA tools reduce manual effort within an organisation but have not been shown to lower system-wide cost, partly because faster provider submission triggers more payer-side scrutiny. The win is real: faster care, less burnout, better documentation. Don't oversell the cost case.

One portal, three personas

Every tenant, every role, one application · who may judge · who may only move paper

There is only one portal

Every practice on the platform and every person inside it signs into the same application at the same address. There is no separate build per customer, no separate app per role, and no separate operator console. A Keycloak token carries two things, and between them they decide everything: a tenant, which settles whose data you see, and a set of scopes, which settles what you may do with it. Both are checked again server-side on every request.

Multi-tenancy and multi-persona are therefore the same mechanism seen from two angles, and neither is a deployment fork. A customer requiring its own infrastructure gets a dedicated or air-gapped deployment of this same codebase with its own realm and keys — a deployment option, not a different product. One person may hold several personas and gets the union of their scopes, so a lean practice never needs extra headcount to satisfy the model.

The authority boundary

Three personas, and the only distinction that matters structurally is clinical authority. Everyone above the line in the diagram below moves paper. Below it sits one persona, and it holds a licence. The agent is permanently above the line, and that is enforced in the type system and the service layer, not by convention.

Tenant Admin and Master Admin are one persona with two reaches. The Vision & Scope splits the admin role in two, and the distinction is real — but it is a matter of scope, not of screens. The ordering physician does not log in: their one action — Get Authorization — is placed by Operations or arrives from the EHR at order-sign, and anything clinical routes to the Clinical Reviewer. The payer is a counterparty reached through connectors, not a tenant of this platform. The patient benefits from faster care; from 1 January 2027 CMS-0057-F requires PA status and history to be exposed through the Patient Access API, consent-gated and through the practice — an API surface, not a login here.

The last row is the point. No persona can issue a determination — those are the payer's acts, arriving through a connector as a PAS ClaimResponse, an X12 278 response, a portal screen or a call outcome, and recorded with actorType: "payer". A denial unconditionally raises a task for the licensed Clinical Reviewer; that routing is a rule, and no threshold, trust mode or kill-switch position changes it.

These are scopes on the session, checked by useSession().can() in the UI and re-checked in the service layer. In production they are Keycloak realm roles mapped to token scopes, with the API checking the scope and tenant_id from the token, and PostgreSQL row-level security enforcing tenancy again underneath.

What the system does

One click · one case · four channels · every branch recorded

The physician clicks Get Authorization. The agent confirms coverage, asks the payer electronically whether authorization is even required, assembles exactly the clinical packet that payer's policy asks for, submits it the cheapest way that payer supports, handles the mid-review document demands, and writes the result back into the chart. Staff see only the exceptions — each one already loaded with the context behind it.

AI capabilities and guardrails

Seven agent roles · what each may not do · the gate · the vector behind the score

The rule, stated once and enforced everywhere

The agent does the paperwork; it never makes the medical judgment. Medical necessity, peer-to-peer and every denial go to a licensed human. In this design that is not a policy statement — it is enforced in code, and there is no path that records a denial without a named licensed reviewer.

What the AI does — and never does

Does

Document extraction

Deterministic (CQL/FHIRPath) first; an LLM with JSON-Schema-constrained output for narrative text. Every fact carries document, span and confidence.

Does

Criteria matching

Per criterion: met / not-met / unclear, with evidence and confidence. The rules engine decides what that means for routing.

Does

Missing-doc detection

Against the matched rule's required list, flagged as agent-, staff- or clinician-resolvable.

Does

Letter drafting

A medical-necessity letter citing only extracted facts, never sent without human approval.

Does

Transcript structuring

Turning a payer call into a structured outcome with a reference number.

Never

Issues a denial

AIRecommendation has three values — submit, gather more, escalate. There is no fourth.

Never

Decides necessity

It reports facts and gaps. A not-met routes to a human; it never produces a refusal.

Never

Files alone

A licensed clinician approves every appeal before filing.

Never

Calls without checking

Automated-caller permission per payer; one- vs two-party recording consent per state, per call.

Never

Recalls a rule

Payer rules come from the versioned engine, not from model memory.

Data models and the canonical case

24 entities · one case · the constraints that encode the product's rules

Every document in the source folder converges on the same claim: API, portal, voice and human are attempts on one case — never separate bots and never separate records. So PriorAuthRequest is the spine. When the waterfall falls through from PAS to a portal to a phone call, the case id does not change; only attempts[] grows. That single decision is what makes the audit trail, the patient journey and the KPI roll-ups work without reconciliation.

A determination is the payer's act. It arrives through a connector — a PAS ClaimResponse, an X12 278 response, a portal screen or a call outcome — and is recorded with actorType: "payer". Decision.decidedByUserId is therefore always undefined: there is no user of ours to attribute it to.

  request_id       uuid NOT NULL DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  case_number      text NOT NULL,

ALTER TABLE prior_auth_request ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;

pa_required boolean is nullable, and NULL means unknown. A three-valued column is the right shape here precisely because the two-valued one would invite a default. Two storage layers sit behind this, linked by provenance: immutable raw (what the source actually sent — the FHIR bundle, the 271, the call transcript) and a derived normalized case model. When a mapping turns out to be wrong, the raw copy is what lets you re-derive rather than re-fetch.

Standards: FHIR, Da Vinci, X12

What the standards cover · what they do not · what CMS requires and when

NexAuthAI's canonical model is FHIR R4–shaped so that adapters translate once, at the edge, and the core never learns a vendor's dialect. Every regulatory API and every Da Vinci implementation guide is R4. R5 is published and R6 is in ballot, but neither is what payers or CMS reference.

The single most load-bearing fact about X12

X12 278 carries no attachments. This is not an implementation shortcut — it is the standard's shape. A 278 submission needs a second motion for documentation: fax, portal upload, or X12 275. The 275/277 claims-attachment compliance date is 26 May 2028, and PA attachments are not finalised. Any plan that assumes 278 transports a clinical packet is wrong, and the waterfall must model documentation as a separate step on that lane.

8

Architecture

Seven layers · seven design rules · the production stack

The prototype is a frontend with a fake service layer. What follows describes the real system that service layer stands in for: TypeScript end to end, a modular monolith over PostgreSQL with row-level security, running a durable state machine with an outbox.

The seven design rules

Rule 1

Canonical case, many channels

API, portal, voice and human are attempts on one case. They never become separate bots or separate records.

Rule 2

Core closed, connectors open

A new EHR or payer implements the adapter contract. The engine does not learn a vendor's dialect.

Rule 3

LLMs reason; rules decide

Agents read, extract, classify and draft. Requirements, thresholds, routing and deadlines come from the versioned rules engine, which the model cannot override.

Rule 4

Durable by default

State changes and outbound events commit in one transaction. Workers are idempotent. Timers survive restarts. Nothing depends on a browser tab staying open.

Rule 5

Two storage layers

Immutable raw and a derived normalized model, linked by provenance. When a mapping is wrong you re-derive rather than re-fetch.

Rule 6

Connections are stateful

Act on active / degraded / failed / refresh-failed / expired / disconnected — not on whether an OAuth flow once finished.

Rule 7

Modular monolith first

One deployable API with clear module boundaries plus separately scaled workers. Split when load or team boundaries demand it, not before.

The platform around the case

Three concerns surround every case and belong to the portal rather than to any one request: who may act (§2), who gets told what, and how it is all paid for.

Notifications — three channels, one payload rule

The same domain events that move a case fan out to a notification service. In-app is the queue itself and is always on. Email (transactional) and web push (a browser service worker, delivered with the dashboard closed) are what leave the building, and each is opt-out per event type, per user.

ChannelWhat it isPreference
In-appThe bell and the notification centreNo toggle — it is the queue
EmailTransactional, to the user's addressOpt-out per event type
Web pushBrowser service worker; needs a permission grant and a stored endpointOpt-out per event type, plus the browser grant

The payload rule does not relax by channel: a notification carries an ID and an event type and nothing else — no patient name, no diagnosis, no procedure code, no payer rationale. The message says a case needs attention and links back into the app, where access is re-checked on arrival. An inbox and a lock screen are the two places PHI must never sit.

Turning a channel off suppresses delivery only. The event still fires, the in-app row still appears and the audit entry is still written — a preference can never make something vanish from the record. Events are role-scoped: a clinical-review request only reaches the Clinical Reviewer, a held submission only reaches Operations, a connection failure or an invoice only reaches Admin.

The $4-per-PA figure in the market models is a TAM/SAM sizing device, as the workbook itself states. It must not leak into pricing.

Every usage record names the case that caused the spend, so an invoice line — “612 voice minutes” — audits down to the exact cases behind it. That also prices the waterfall honestly: an electronic check costs a fraction of a cent, a portal session cents, a voice call dollars. Cheapest-channel-first is a margin decision as well as a speed one. Settling an invoice is a Stripe payment intent against the stored method, and the act writes invoice.paid to the audit log like any other.

Security, privacy, compliance

Business associate · four isolation layers · RBAC plus ABAC · SOC 2

Tenant isolation — four layers, on purpose

RBAC for the coarse grain, ABAC for the rest

One portal, three personas. Staff / Operations, Clinical Reviewer and Admin all sign into the same application; the navigation, the routes and the rows each call returns are derived from the token, not from a per-role build. One person may hold several personas and gets the union of their scopes.

RBAC: Keycloak realm roles map to token scopes — request:read, clinical:attest, policy:write, connector:write, tenant:manage, billing:manage, audit:read.

ABAC handles what roles cannot express:

  • The admin reach: tenant:manage with adminScope == "tenant" returns one tenant; with adminScope == "platform" it returns all of them. Same endpoint, same screen, different row set — this is how Tenant Admin and Master Admin became one persona rather than two.
  • A clinical user's attestation and appeal approval require isLicensedReviewer == true on the linked Provider record, not merely the scope.
  • Each agent role gets only the tools and fields it needs — field-level policy, Cedar-style. The Coverage agent cannot read clinical notes; the Clinical agent cannot call the submission tool.

The Admin persona has no PHI scopes at all, on either reach — which the prototype implements literally, by omitting request:read from that persona's scope list. A platform admin can see that a tenant's exception rate is climbing and cannot open a single case behind it.

The connector layer

One contract, many adapters · five layers · four payer tiers

A mid-size practice deals with dozens of payers. Each has a different portal, a different form, a different fax number, and — increasingly — a different partial implementation of the same FHIR standard. Point-to-point integration means n × m bridges, every one of which breaks independently. The alternative is a canonical model in the middle: adapters translate at the edge, once, and the core never learns a vendor's dialect.

The adapter contract

export interface Connector {
  describe(): ConnectorManifest;
  test(conn: ConnectionRef): Promise<HealthResult>;

  checkEligibility?(conn, req: EligibilityRequest):   Promise<ConnectorResult<EligibilityResult>>;
  isPARequired?(conn, req: RequirementRequest):       Promise<ConnectorResult<RequirementResult>>;
  getQuestionnaire?(conn, req: QuestionnaireRequest): Promise<ConnectorResult<Questionnaire>>;
  fetchDocuments?(conn, req: DocumentRequest):        Promise<ConnectorResult<ClinicalDocument[]>>;
  submitPA?(conn, req: SubmissionRequest):            Promise<ConnectorResult<SubmissionReceipt>>;
  getStatus?(conn, externalRef: string):              Promise<ConnectorResult<StatusResult>>;
  respondToRFI?(conn, req: RFIResponseRequest):       Promise<ConnectorResult<SubmissionReceipt>>;
  writeBack?(conn, req: WriteBackRequest):            Promise<ConnectorResult<void>>;
  subscribeToUpdates?(conn, handler: (e: ConnectorEvent) => void): () => void;
}
Decision 1

Everything optional but describe and test

Capability genuinely varies — most payers in 2026 do not implement CRD, DTR and PAS. An adapter forced to stub seven methods would lie about what it can do. The manifest declares the truth and the route resolver reads it.

Decision 2

Every method returns an envelope

{ ok, data?, error?, meta } — and meta carries connector id, instance id, external ref, latency and timestamp. That block is what gets written to the case ledger.

Decision 3

A shared error taxonomy

auth_expired · rate_limited · payer_unavailable · validation_failed · ui_changed · timeout · mapping_missing · not_supported · unknown, each with retryable. The waterfall decides from the taxonomy, never the vendor's wording.

Decision 4

Idempotency is the adapter's job

A repeat of the same idempotencyKey is the same submission, not a second one. This, plus inquire-before-submit, is what makes a zero-duplicate criterion achievable.

The source folder asserts “auto-provisioned in minutes” for a registry match and prices 3 hours per electronic route and 4 per portal. Neither figure has evidence behind it. Measure the first three real onboardings and replace that table.

And the number that actually moves is not engineering. Vendor program enrollment and per-customer activation are gated by other organisations' calendars. A 10-day Epic connector can sit for six weeks waiting on a customer's security review. The step teams skip and regret is the data-quality assessment: how much of this site's coverage is coded, what their order codes look like, what proportion of problem lists carry codes. If a site's problem lists are 40% uncoded, DTR pre-population will not work there — and you want to know that before you promise a touchless rate.

Open questions and assumptions

Ten decisions needing an owner · eight gaps · eight labelled assumptions

The two that matter most

G1 — there are no payer-side requirements anywhere in the source. Every client and consulting document is provider-side. Payer intake reviewers, clinical reviewers, work queues, reason codes and decision forms appear only in the research. An earlier draft built them anyway, as labelled assumptions. The eight questions that actually block work

  1. Pricing model — subscription, metered, or hybrid? Blocks billing design and every commercial section.
  2. Does NexAuthAI serve payers too? The brief implied yes; no source document does. Answered for now: no — the product is provider-side and payers are counterparties reached through connectors. Reopening it changes the product's shape, go-to-market and compliance posture fundamentally, so it should be a deliberate decision rather than an inference.
  3. Which EHR, which clearinghouse, which payers, which CPT family? The whole integration plan is placeholder until these land.
  4. Acceptance thresholds for leaving Shadow mode — who signs, and at what measured accuracy?
  5. Deployment mode — multi-tenant SaaS, or dedicated / air-gapped?
  6. Is n8n in or out? Affects both the onboarding price and the compliance story.
  7. Retention period — the Vision & Scope defers to “the practice's existing HIPAA policy”; the blueprint recommends ≥6 years and says “validate with counsel”. Currently unowned.
  8. Who owns the payer-requirement matrix? Named as the single biggest source of stale-data error, and assigned to nobody.

In one paragraph

An order for an MRI arrives — from the EHR at order-sign, or placed by Operations with one click of Get Authorization. NexAuthAI opens a case, confirms coverage, and asks the payer electronically whether CPT 72148 even needs authorization — if not, it logs the proof with a reference number and stops. If it does, the agent assembles exactly the packet that payer's policy requires, escalating to a clinician only when real clinical evidence is missing, then submits it the cheapest way that payer supports: electronic, then portal, then a phone call, then a human — without ever creating a duplicate.

When the payer asks for more mid-review, it fetches just that document and resupplies it without starting over. The outcome comes back, the authorization number and valid dates are written into the chart, and the practice sees only the result or the one exception that genuinely needs a person. Every step is written once to an audit trail that can be replayed later.

And the agent never, at any point, decides whether the care was necessary.
